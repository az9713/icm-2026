Learning in Games with Carryover Effects — Éva Tardos

Field: algorithmic game theory. Specifically: what happens to the quality of a shared system when every participant is running a learning algorithm, and the system has state that their past actions created.

Difficulty against your background: split. The mathematics is almost all yours already — a Lyapunov function with negative drift, a geometric series, a two-case counting argument, some Chernoff bounds. Call it 2/5. The vocabulary is not yours: Nash equilibrium, price of anarchy, no-regret learning, coarse correlated equilibrium, smooth games. That is a real gap and it gets a real bridge section. Call it 3/5.

What this tutorial builds: the price of anarchy in one page; no-regret learning as a behavioural assumption rather than an algorithm; why price-of-anarchy bounds survive being handed to learners; the strategic queuing model and its factor-of-2 stability theorem, with the drift proof sketched at honest depth; and the two ways no-regret learning is now known to be the wrong assumption.

1. What is at stake

Tardos opens by naming an agenda change, and it is worth taking literally because the whole hour is downstream of it.

"Maybe early even in my life the agenda for computer science was that there is a problem, here's the input, and you're supposed to generate the best output you know how to generate. But I think that's not really a fair agenda anymore, because there's a lot of systems we share."

The old agenda is the one you were trained in: a well-posed problem, an objective, an algorithm, a guarantee. The new agenda is that the input to your problem is being generated, right now, by other people solving their problems on the same shared resource — and increasingly not by people at all, but by their software.

Her illustration is the tragedy of the commons: an English village where everyone may graze a cow on the common green. Each individual is correct that adding one more cow helps them. Collectively the grass runs out. The measure of the damage is what Christos Papadimitriou named the price of anarchy.

Then the sentence that sets up the second half:

"What I'm thinking about is learning in a setting when other people are also learning, and you're interacting with other learners — and what happens as a result of all of us using AI, which is what we're doing."

So the talk has two movements. First: the classical price-of-anarchy programme, and the happy discovery that its bounds survive when you replace the equilibrium assumption with a much weaker assumption about learning. Second: that the discovery has a hidden hypothesis — the game must not evolve — and that most real systems violate it. The rest of the talk is about what happens when it does.

2. Your anchor

2.1 The main one: this is a Lyapunov argument with the force law removed

The technical core of the talk — the queuing theorem — has exactly the shape you already use to prove a dynamical system does not blow up.

You know the move. You have a system whose state you cannot integrate. You exhibit a non-negative function V of the state, you show that whenever V is large its time derivative is negative, and you conclude V stays bounded. Lyapunov for ODEs; Foster's criterion for Markov chains; free-energy decrease in statistical mechanics. Same instrument.

Tardos's proof is precisely this. V is the total age of all undelivered packets. The theorem she borrows — Pemantle and Rosenthal, 1999 — is the stochastic version: a non-negative random sequence with negative expected drift once it exceeds a threshold, plus a moment-regularity condition, stays bounded in expectation forever.

What makes it a game theory result rather than a queuing theory result is the one thing that is missing: there is no equation of motion. She does not know what algorithm any participant is running. Real routers use "much simpler algorithms than what we have good guarantees for". She refuses to specify them.

So the negative-drift estimate cannot come from dynamics. It has to come from a hypothesis about the agents — an inequality they are assumed to satisfy, whatever they are actually doing. That inequality is the no-regret condition (§3.3). The entire architecture of the talk is:

Lyapunov function + behavioural inequality in place of a force law → boundedness.

And the factor of 2 in her theorem is not decoration. It is exactly the slack the drift computation needs in order to close. §5 shows where it enters.

2.2 The secondary one: a stationary point of the parts is not a stationary point of the whole

The price of anarchy measures a gap you already have a name for.

Write down a system with n participants, each with their own objective u_i, all depending on the joint configuration a = (a_1, …, a_n). A Nash equilibrium is a configuration where each u_i is optimal in its own coordinate block, holding the others fixed. That is coordinate-wise optimality — a fixed point of the best-response map. The social optimum maximises Σ_i u_i over all a jointly.

These are different variational problems. Coordinate-wise stationarity of the individual objectives is not stationarity of the sum, because ∂u_i/∂a_i = 0 for every i says nothing about ∂(Σ_j u_j)/∂a_i, which carries the cross terms ∂u_j/∂a_i — the externalities. The price of anarchy is the size of that discrepancy, in the worst case.

If you want a one-line version: an equilibrium is where nobody's own gradient points anywhere; an optimum is where the total gradient vanishes. The externality is the difference.

2.3 The one that should genuinely surprise you

Here is the fact I would put in front of any optimizer. In optimization, enlarging the feasible set cannot make the optimum worse. Add a variable, add an edge, relax a constraint: the value can only improve. It is the most reliable monotonicity you own.

At equilibrium, it is false. Adding a free, instantaneous, zero-cost option to a network can make every single participant strictly worse off, with no way for any of them to individually recover. That is Braess's paradox (§4.1), and Tardos says it is the example that convinced her, and a generation, that something real was going on.

The reason is that an equilibrium is not the optimum of anything. It is a fixed point. Fixed points have no monotonicity theorem.

3. The bridge

Five definitions. Each one deforms something you have.

3.1 Nash equilibrium

Notation, hers. There are n players. Player i chooses an action a_i. The vector a = (a_1, …, a_n) is the joint action. Player i's utility is u_i(a) — note that it depends on everyone's action, not just their own. Write a_{−i} for "everything except player i's component", so that (x, a_{−i}) means "player i plays x, everyone else plays what they play in a".

a is a Nash equilibrium if for every player i and every alternative action x:

\[u_i(a) \;\ge\; u_i(x,\, a_{-i}).\]

In words: no one, acting alone, can improve by changing their mind. Tardos calls this inequality "a no-regret inequality" from the podium and it is the right way to read it — player i does not regret not having played x.

That is all it is. It is a fixed point of the best-response correspondence. John Nash proved one always exists if you allow randomised (mixed) actions.

3.2 The price of anarchy

Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou, 1999; the name is Papadimitriou's.

\[\text{PoA} \;=\; \frac{\text{cost at the worst Nash equilibrium}}{\text{cost at the social optimum}}.\]

Tardos notes from the podium that she prefers to write it this way round — cost on top — so that the price of anarchy is always ≥ 1 and bigger is worse. If you are working with utilities instead of costs you flip it. Nothing deep, but you will see both in the literature.

3.3 No-regret learning — the definition to actually absorb

This is the pivot of the talk. Everything after it depends on it.

The situation is now a repeated game: at each time step t = 1, …, T every player picks an action a_i^t, and the joint action a^t determines everyone's utility. Nobody knows who else is playing, or what they are doing.

Player i satisfies the no-regret condition if

\[\sum_{t=1}^{T} u_i\big(a^t\big) \;\ge\; \max_{x} \sum_{t=1}^{T} u_i\big(x,\, a^t_{-i}\big) \;-\; o(T).\]

Read the two sides. On the left: what you actually got, summed over time. On the right: what you would have got had you played the single fixed action x at every step, against the same realised behaviour of everybody else, maximised over x — minus an error term that grows more slowly than T.

Three things to notice, in order of importance.

First: this is the same inequality as Nash, with a time average in front of it. Put the Nash condition and this one side by side and the only differences are the sum over t and the slack term. That is not an accident and it is the engine of §4.7.

Second: it is a hypothesis about the agent, not an algorithm. Tardos is emphatic:

"I want to think of this as what I like to call a behavioural assumption. I don't want to mandate that you use a particular learning algorithm, because for all those softwares I actually have some guesses but don't know what they do."

Her justification for it being a reasonable assumption is a negative one, and it is a good one: if there was a single fixed action that would have done much better than what you did, and you never noticed, you did not learn. Anything worth calling a learning algorithm should clear that bar.

Third: why the o(T) is unavoidable. At the start you knew nothing. A guarantee with no slack would demand clairvoyance on step one. The achievable rate is o(T) — Tardos says "roughly square root of t over time t", i.e. regret O(√T), so average regret O(1/√T) → 0. Algorithms achieving it are simple and old: watch the past, randomise, and put more weight on actions that did well. Multiplicative weights, in the version you may know as Hedge; the bandit-feedback version EXP3.P is what the queuing papers actually invoke.

Her illustration of what no-regret does and does not demand is worth keeping. You commute daily. One road is bad in sun, good in rain; the other the reverse. No-regret does not require you to learn the conditional rule — that would be a much stronger demand. It requires only that you do at least as well as the better of the two fixed choices. "If there's a single solution that's really good, I'm hoping that you'll learn to do this, or do even better by alternating in interesting ways."

Evidence that people and machines satisfy it. She reports data from Bing sponsored search bidding around 2012, from Google ad auctions, and from human subject experiments. (The Bing analysis is Nekipelov, Syrgkanis and Tardos, Econometrics for Learning Agents, EC 2015 — the whole point of that paper is to do inference on bidders assuming no-regret instead of assuming equilibrium.) But she is candid about why she likes it:

"To be honest, because I'm more a mathematician and because this is a maths conference, I love it because it's a behavioural assumption that I can beautifully use in theory."

3.4 Smoothness, and "the proofs only use one inequality"

Tardos says from the podium that the price-of-anarchy proofs "really use only one inequality" — namely, that at equilibrium nobody wants to deviate to the optimal solution. Not to any of the other actions. Just that one.

She does not write it down. The published form is Roughgarden's smoothness framework, and it is short enough to give in full, because it is the load-bearing structure of the entire first half.

A cost-minimisation game is (λ, μ)-smooth if for all pairs of joint actions s and s*:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n} C_i\big(s^*_i,\, s_{-i}\big) \;\le\; \lambda \cdot \mathrm{cost}(s^*) \;+\; \mu \cdot \mathrm{cost}(s)\]

where cost(·) is the social objective and satisfies cost(s) ≤ Σ_i C_i(s).

The left-hand side is the strange entangled object: "what each player would pay if they alone switched to their part of s*, against everyone else still playing s". Smoothness says that entangled thing can be disentangled into the only two quantities anyone cares about. Note that this is a statement about all pairs s, s* — it is pure algebra about the game, with no equilibrium anywhere in it.

Now the proof, in one line, that a Nash equilibrium s has cost at most λ/(1−μ) times optimal:

\[\mathrm{cost}(s) \;\le\; \sum_i C_i(s) \;\overset{(\star)}{\le}\; \sum_i C_i(s^*_i, s_{-i}) \;\le\; \lambda\,\mathrm{cost}(s^*) + \mu\,\mathrm{cost}(s)\]

and rearrange. Step (⋆) is the only place the equilibrium hypothesis is used, and it is used exactly once per player, with the deviation being that player's component of the optimum. That is Tardos's "one inequality", made precise.

Worked constant: atomic selfish routing with affine cost functions is (5/3, 1/3)-smooth, giving PoA ≤ (5/3)/(1 − 1/3) = 5/2.

3.5 Stability of a queuing system

You have the M/M/1 intuition already, and she uses it as the baseline: one queue, one server, packets arrive at rate λ, get served at rate μ. If λ > μ you are lost. The classical theorem says λ < μ is enough.

"Stable" here will mean strongly stable: for every fixed r ≥ 0 there is a constant C_r, not depending on t, with E[(total queue size at time t)^r] ≤ C_r. Not just finite in expectation — all moments bounded, uniformly in time. That is the conclusion Pemantle– Rosenthal delivers.

4. The talk, rebuilt

4.1 Braess's paradox

(Reconstructed figure. The network was on a slide and is invisible to the captions; every number below is spoken in the transcript and I checked that they are mutually consistent. Dietrich Braess, 1968.)

One hundred cars want to get from A to B. Two routes:

                C
              ↗   ↘
   x/100 hrs ↗       ↘ 1 hr
            ↗           ↘
           A             B
            ↘           ↗
      1 hr   ↘         ↗ x/100 hrs
              ↘       ↗
                D

The edges A→C and D→B are congestion sensitive: if x cars use one, it takes x/100 hours. One car crosses almost instantly; a hundred cars take a full hour. The edges C→B and A→D take a flat 1 hour regardless.

Equilibrium without the tunnel. The two routes are symmetric, so the cars split 50/50. Each route costs 50/100 + 1 = 1.5 hours. Nobody can improve by switching — switching would make their new route carry 51 cars, costing 0.51 + 1 = 1.51. Stable.

Now add a tunnel C→D that is free and instantaneous.

The new route A→C→D→B exists. At the old 50/50 flow, one driver who deviates onto it pays 51/100 + 0 + 51/100 ≈ 1.02 hours instead of 1.5. Strictly better. So they switch. So does everyone.

Equilibrium with the tunnel. All 100 cars on A→C→D→B: 100/100 + 0 + 100/100 = 2 hours. Check that it is an equilibrium: deviating back to A→C→B costs 100/100 + 1 = 2 hours as well. No improvement. Stable.

Everyone was at 1.5 hours. A free improvement to the network was added. Everyone is now at 2 hours, and no individual can escape.

Tardos's reaction, which is the correct one:

"The paradox is that we put in an extra option which in classical optimization can't possibly hurt. An extra option can only make life better. But here it made life worse."

The social optimum on the augmented network is still 1.5 hours — just don't use the tunnel. So the price of anarchy of this instance is 2 / 1.5 = 4/3, which is exactly the tight worst-case bound for nonatomic routing with affine latencies (Roughgarden & Tardos, JACM 2002). Braess is not merely an example; it is the extremal example.

(One honesty note: the analysis above treats the 100 cars as a continuum — each car's own contribution to congestion is ignored when it computes its route. That is the standard nonatomic model and it is what the 4/3 bound refers to. Tardos says "100 cars" from the podium.)

4.2 The move that recurs all hour: measure the resource, not the damage

Her first theorem is joint with Tim Roughgarden, and she is careful to say it is not a price-of-anarchy statement. It is better, and the reason it is better is the through-line of the whole lecture.

Bicriteria theorem (Roughgarden & Tardos, How Bad Is Selfish Routing?, JACM 2002). In any network with continuous non-decreasing latency functions, and for any set of source–sink pairs with any traffic rates — Boston to New York and everyone else at the same time — the total delay of the selfish equilibrium at traffic rate r is no worse than the total delay of the centrally designed optimum at traffic rate 2r.

Read what that buys you. A price-of-anarchy number tells you how bad things are. This tells you what to do: you cannot make drivers take the routes you choose, and you could not compute the routes for the whole internet anyway — so stop trying to coordinate and buy capacity instead. Doubling gets you back to where central planning would have put you.

"Certainly coordinating is of course painful. I can't tell drivers which way to drive. And maybe I could tell the internet which way to send the packets, but it's too complicated to do. So what the theorem says is: just up the capacities."

Hold onto this. Every result in the talk has this shape:

Setting Resource needed to buy off the lack of coordination
Selfish routing, any network (2002) 2× the traffic rate
Strategic queuing, no buffers, no-regret learners (2020) 2× the server capacity
Same, but players maximise long-run rate rather than being no-regret (2021) e/(e−1) ≈ 1.58×
Strategic queuing, single-packet buffers, no-regret learners (2025) 3×, with a lower bound of 2×

She never asks "how bad is it". She asks "what do I have to buy". That is the transferable reframe and §7 comes back to it.

4.3 Two objections to Nash equilibrium

Having set up the price of anarchy properly, she then undermines its foundation, on purpose.

"In this Nash equilibrium they somehow magically found this stable solution. How did they find it?"

Objection 1 — computational. Computing a Nash equilibrium in a general game is PPAD-complete: hard, under the standard assumptions. She credits Constantinos Daskalakis, who won the prize for it at ICM 2018 in Rio — awarded then as the Rolf Nevanlinna Prize, and the award is now called the IMU Abacus Medal, which is the name Tardos uses. She adds, with evident pleasure, that she gave his laudation at that congress.

Objection 2 — informational, and she puts it in red on the slide. To play a best response you must know who else is in the game and what they are doing.

"Good luck as you're driving home at the end of this conference from Philly to wherever you live — good luck in knowing who else is on the road and which way they are driving. You don't actually tend to know this."

The second objection is the more damaging one and it is the one that motivates everything that follows. Even if you could compute a Nash equilibrium, you have no basis for assuming anyone is at one.

4.4 What people hoped would happen, and why it doesn't

The idea of learning in repeated games is older than the computer science of it. Brown (1951) and Julia Robinson (1951) studied fictitious play: look at the empirical distribution of everyone's past actions, and best-respond to it.

Note the built-in incoherence: you change your strategy while assuming nobody else does.

They were not modelling real life. They imagined it as pre-play — a practice run you perform before the real game, which delivers an equilibrium you then adopt. Robinson proved it converges in two-player zero-sum games (rock–paper–scissors, chess-like win/lose situations).

And essentially nowhere else. Tardos: "unfortunately it almost always doesn't work. It works on those games, a couple other lucky games, but mostly this kind of thing is not good enough to find any stable solution."

Then the empirical nail. She shows a plot of real advertiser bids on Bing search advertising, from around 2012. They do not converge. They oscillate, permanently — because the advertisers are running sophisticated optimisers, either the platform's own or third-party bid managers, and those optimisers keep moving.

So: stop hoping for convergence. Assume only the no-regret inequality of §3.3, which is a statement about the whole trajectory rather than about where it ends up.

4.5 The lift: price-of-anarchy bounds survive learning, for free

Now the payoff, and it is elegant.

Recall §3.4: the equilibrium hypothesis is used exactly once, per player, in the form "you don't want to deviate to your component of the optimum". No-regret gives you exactly that same inequality, in time-averaged form, with an o(T) slack.

So you substitute. The price-of-anarchy proof does not care.

"What's beautiful in the no-regret learning is that we have the analog: you don't want to deviate to this a* strategy. And all you have to do to extend the proofs is just substitute this inequality. It's pretty simple."

That is Roughgarden's extension theorem (Intrinsic Robustness of the Price of Anarchy, STOC 2009 / JACM 2015): every price-of-anarchy bound proved by a smoothness argument applies automatically to the time-average of any no-regret play sequence, and more generally to every coarse correlated equilibrium — a distribution σ over joint actions such that E_{s∼σ}[C_i(s)] ≤ E_{s∼σ}[C_i(s'i, s)] for every i and every deviation s'_i. The proof is five lines of linearity of expectation applied to the chain in §3.4.

She flags two details that matter:

  • There is a small error term. The o(T) regret slack survives into the bound, degrading it gracefully. Fine.
  • You never need to know the optimum. This is the part worth pausing on. No-regret says you do not regret any fixed action in hindsight. In particular you do not regret the optimal one — even though nobody, least of all the learner, ever computed it. The hypothesis is universally quantified, so the proof gets to instantiate it at whatever comparison it needs.

Tardos also notes an extension of her own: the guarantee survives a changing population of players — it is not the same drivers every day. That is Lykouris, Syrgkanis and Tardos, Learning and Efficiency in Games with Dynamic Population, SODA 2016, whose main technical tool is, surprisingly, differential privacy.

This programme ran for roughly fifteen years and it worked. And then:

4.6 The cheat

"Unfortunately there is a cheat, or a critical assumption. It's basically assumed that the game you're talking about is like rock–paper–scissors. When we play rock–paper–scissors the game doesn't evolve as we're playing it. There's no carryover effect."

Every result above assumes the rounds are independent — that the only thing crossing from round t to round t+1 is what the learners remember. The game itself resets.

Whether that is reasonable is a question about time scale, and she answers it precisely:

  • Morning rush hour, day to day: reasonable. However bad Monday was, by Tuesday morning everyone has gone home. The board is cleared.
  • Packet routing, millisecond to millisecond: not reasonable. If packets were jammed at a router one millisecond ago, they are still sitting there now.
  • Traffic, minute to minute: also not reasonable. The 5:00 jam is still the 5:05 jam.

And her "killer example": budgeted auctions. You spend money; later you have less money. The game you are playing tomorrow is the game you spent your way into today. (That thread is its own literature — Balseiro & Gur; Fikioris & Tardos — and she does not pursue it here.)

So the object of study becomes: repeated games in which the state carries over, and the state is the residue of everyone's past success and failure. She takes packet queues as the concrete case.

4.7 The queuing model

Joint work with Jason Gaitonde, now on the faculty at Duke.

The physical story first. Packets arrive because someone sent an email. Data-centre servers try to handle them. When things get crowded the server drops the packet — in reality it simply never sends an acknowledgement, and the sender, seeing no acknowledgement, resends. Tardos phrases it as "the packet got returned to the sender", which is the right abstraction.

The formal model (Gaitonde–Tardos, EC 2020):

  1. Discrete time steps t = 0, 1, 2, ….
  2. n queues. At each step, queue i receives a new packet with probability λ_i (Bernoulli, independent).
  3. m servers. Server j succeeds at serving a packet with probability μ_j.
  4. One send per step. Each queue may send one packet to one server per step. This is a real constraint, not a modelling convenience — a router has one send head.
  5. One accept per step. Each server can deal with at most one packet per step. If several arrive, the others are dropped and returned to their senders.
  6. Age priority. Packets carry a timestamp, and when a server must choose among several arriving packets it serves the oldest. Tardos flags this as a fact about the modern internet: "this wasn't true 20 years ago. They realised that trying to deal with older packets makes things infinitely more efficient."

Point 6 looks like a detail. It is load-bearing; see §4.11.

The question is her usual one. Not how bad is it — but:

"How much more capacity do I have to give the system to be able to handle it, even though these queues obviously don't coordinate, because it's too complicated?"

4.8 The coordinated baseline

Before you can price the lack of coordination you must know what coordination would buy. Sort both vectors in decreasing order: λ_1 ≥ λ_2 ≥ … and μ_1 ≥ μ_2 ≥ ….

Theorem (Gaitonde–Tardos, EC 2020, Theorem 2.1). The system is stable under some centralised scheduling policy if and only if for every k = 1, …, n:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{k} \mu_i \;>\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i.\]

(The paper's statement carries a preprocessing step: delete a maximal equal prefix of 1's from both vectors first. That handles the degenerate case of rate-1 servers matched to rate-1 queues.)

Tardos reads it aloud and the reading is the point. Take k = 1: the busiest queue must have some server that can handle it alone, because it can only send to one place at a time — so λ_1 < μ_1, "otherwise even if he was alone he was hosed, there's nothing he can do". Take k = 2: the two busiest queues can between them occupy at most two servers per step, so the two fastest servers had better be able to absorb them. And so on for every k.

This is the multi-server generalisation of the M/M/1 condition λ < μ, and it is a Hall-type condition — n inequalities, one per prefix, because of the one-send-per-step bottleneck. When it holds she calls the system centrally feasible. It is not hard to prove; it is just nice to read.

4.9 The theorem

Theorem (Gaitonde–Tardos, EC 2020, Theorem 3.1 with Assumption 3.1). Suppose the system would still be centrally feasible with all service rates halved — formally, that there is η > 0 with

\[\tfrac12 (1-\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_i \;\ge\; \sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i \qquad \text{for all } k,\]

and suppose every queue runs a learning algorithm satisfying the no-regret condition with high probability on long windows. Then the system is strongly stable: the expected number of packets in the system, and every moment of it, stays bounded for all time.

The price of decentralisation is a factor of 2 in capacity. Give the system twice what a knowledgeable central scheduler would need, let everyone learn selfishly, and it holds.

Tardos draws the parallel to her 2002 theorem with Roughgarden herself — same factor of 2, same shape of conclusion — and immediately says the mechanism is completely different. That is worth trusting: the 2002 result is a static convexity-flavoured argument about flows; this one is a stochastic drift argument. The 2 arrives twice by coincidence of the arithmetic, not by a common cause.

4.10 The proof, at the depth she gave it

She gives about three slides. Here is the same argument with the pieces named.

Step 1 — the drift theorem. Pemantle and Rosenthal (1999). Let X_1, X_2, … be non-negative random variables such that:

  1. (Negative drift when large.) There are constants α, β > 0 with E[X_{t+1} − X_t | ℱ_t, X_t ≥ β] < −α.
  2. (Moment regularity.) There is p > 2 and θ > 0 with E[|X_{t+1} − X_t|^p | ℱ_t] ≤ θ for any history.

Then for every 0 < r < p − 1 there is a constant M, independent of t, with E[X_t^r] ≤ M.

Tardos describes it exactly as you would: numbers going up and down, sufficiently regular, and pushed downward whenever they get high. "Obviously these are non-negative integers, so when it's low, like at zero, it's going to grow — I know. But the property they need is that when it goes sufficiently high, in expectation it will come down." She notes it is "not even that easy to prove" and does not prove it.

Step 2 — the potential function. From the podium: Φ_τ is the number of packets of age τ or older, and the potential is Σ_τ Φ_τ. Since a packet of age a is counted in Φ_1, …, Φ_a, that sum is the total age of all packets in the system.

(What the paper actually uses is close but not identical, and the difference is worth knowing. Gaitonde–Tardos track only the age T^i_t of the oldest packet in each queue, via a coupling they call the "dual process" — instead of generating arrivals as a Bernoulli stream, the gap to the next packet is drawn as a Geometric(λ_i) at the moment the current one clears. The potential is Φ(T_t) = Σ_i λ_i T^i_t (T^i_t − 1), and Pemantle–Rosenthal is applied not to Φ but to √Φ sampled every w steps. The relation to the podium version is Q^i_t ≈ λ_i T^i_t, so λ_i (T^i)²/2 is roughly the total age of queue i's packets. Treat the podium version as the honest picture and the paper's as the version that survives contact with the moment condition — the square root is there because it is the aging of the whole system by one step per step that must have controlled increments.)

Step 3 — bounded moments. Technical, and she says so: "the sufficiently regular is a technical condition and it's easy to prove". Fine.

Step 4 — the negative drift. This is the whole content, and it is a two-case argument.

Fix a long window of w steps and a cutoff age τ. Let Q be the set of queues holding packets older than τ, and let S be the |Q| best servers. Now ask what those servers did during the window.

Case A — the old queues kept those servers busy at least half the time.

Then the number of old packets cleared is at least about half of Σ_{j∈S} μ_j per unit time. New packets arrive at rate Σ_{i∈Q} λ_i. And the hypothesis is precisely that ½ Σμ > Σλ with room to spare — that is what the factor of 2 was for. So more old packets leave than new ones arrive. The potential falls.

Case B — the old queues did not. Those servers sat idle (from the point of view of an old packet) at least half the time.

Then no-regret fires. Those queues hold the oldest packets in the system, so they have priority at every server. Had any of them sent to one of the good servers, it would have been served — the slot was there. So the fixed strategy "always send to that server" would have cleared a great many packets. The no-regret condition says the queue did at least that well, minus o(w). So it cleared that many packets somewhere else. The potential falls anyway.

In Tardos's words:

"Either these queues are smart enough that half the time they're keeping these servers busy — that's cool, because I had a factor of two slack. Or they're too dumb: here are the best ones and they're not sending it there. Here is when no-regret comes in. Wait — these top ones were empty half of the time. Why didn't they send it there? They could have sent it there. They would have gotten served. If they have no regret, that means they're getting this much service someplace else. That's also cool with me."

She adds the honest caveat: it must hold for every cutoff τ simultaneously, and "I have to be a little bit more careful with the error bands that I'm hiding under the rock here."

Why the 2 is exactly right. Look at the case split. It is "busy at least half the time" versus "idle at least half the time" — a partition that only works because the two halves cover everything. The 2 is the constant in that partition. She says so: "this suggests that the factor of two is tight here. Somehow I need it to be either busy half the time or open half the time." And it is: the paper's Theorem 3.3 constructs systems satisfying the feasibility condition with ½ + o(1) in place of ½ on which every queue has zero regret on all but finitely many windows and the system is nonetheless unstable.

4.11 The detail that is not a detail: age priority

Look back at model point 6 — servers serve the oldest arriving packet. Case B of the drift argument used it directly: the old queues would have been served had they sent to the idle server, precisely because their packets have priority.

Remove it and the theorem does not merely lose constants. Gaitonde–Tardos Theorem 2.2: in the alternative model without timestamps, for large enough n there is a centrally feasible system of n queues and n servers that stays feasible even when λ is scaled up by Ω(n^{1/3}), in which all queues can be at a Nash equilibrium at every step — and the system is still unstable.

So without age priority, no sub-polynomial capacity factor suffices at all. The factor of 2 is not a property of no-regret learning; it is a property of no-regret learning plus a scheduling discipline that rewards patience. §7 comes back to this, because it is the single most directly actionable thing in the lecture.

4.12 Buffers change everything, and cost a factor of 3

Tardos then says she was cheating, and identifies the cheat herself: real servers are not memoryless. They have a small buffer — "usually very small, like three or four" — and a packet that arrives when the server is busy can wait rather than being dropped.

The extension is a 2025 paper with, in her words, great collaborators: Ariana Abel, Yoav Kolumbus, Jeronimo Martin Duque, Cristian Palma Foster and Tardos, arXiv:2502.08898. For mathematical tractability the buffer holds exactly one packet. A server rejects everything when its buffer is full; otherwise it accepts one arriving packet, chosen uniformly at random among those that arrived; then it processes with probability μ_j. Feedback reaches the sender when the packet is accepted into the buffer, not when it is processed.

Three consequences, and she presents them in the right order.

(a) The coordinated baseline collapses — in the good direction. The prefix condition of §4.8 is no longer necessary. Her example: one queue with λ = 1/2, facing three servers each with μ = 1/4. The no-buffer condition demands λ_1 < μ_1, i.e. 1/2 < 1/4. Hopeless. With buffers, round-robin works: leave a packet in server 1's buffer, spend the next steps filling servers 2 and 3, and by the time you come back to server 1 it has probably finished.

(Reconstructed arithmetic, my own, in her example's numbers: cycling over k servers each of rate μ, you return to a given server k steps later, and it is still busy with probability (1−μ)^k. So acceptance probability is 1 − (1−μ)^k = 1 − (3/4)³ = 37/64 ≈ 0.578 > 1/2 = λ. Stable. What would verify this is the simulation section of the paper, which runs EXP3 on exactly this family.)

She puts the size of the effect strongly: without buffers you needed the prefix condition; with even a single-packet buffer "the capacity of the system infinitely went up".

(b) But the buffered system is harder, not easier, and the clean condition is gone. The only necessary condition she is confident of is the crude one, Σ_i λ_i < Σ_j μ_j — and it is not sufficient. The paper's Lemma 1 gives the counterexample: one queue and two servers each with μ = 1/2 needs λ < 23/24 < 1 even with full coordination. Buffers make the acceptance probability depend on the state of the buffers, so it is no longer just μ_j, and the elegant linear-programming-duality characterisation of the no-buffer case (Fu et al. 2022) does not survive.

(c) The theorem. (Abel–Kolumbus–Martin Duque–Palma Foster–Tardos, Theorem 1.)

Assuming Σ_i λ_i < ⅓ Σ_j μ_j, and λ_i < ½ for all i, and all queues use learning guaranteeing low regret with high probability, the system remains stable.

Factor of 3, against a baseline of Σλ < Σμ. The potential function is different and simpler than the no-buffer one — Φ = Σ_i (N_i − (½λ_i + 2δ)T)^+, where N_i is queue i's size at the start of the window — but the two-case structure of §4.10 is unchanged: their Lemma 2 handles servers busy more than half the time, their Lemma 3 the servers idle more than half the time, where no-regret fires.

Tardos is visibly unhappy about the 3 and says so: the worst example she can produce needs only a factor of 2, so the gap is open. Note the accounting though: you pay 3 against a much weaker baseline and you got an unbounded increase in what the system can handle. That is a good trade.

4.13 The lower bound, and why it is embarrassing

The example that proves you need at least 2 is short enough to state completely, and it is the sharpest single thing in the talk.

(Abel et al., Theorem 3.) One queue with arrival rate λ = 1/2. It faces k servers, each of capacity 1/n. Total service capacity is k/n. The queue picks a uniformly random server at each step — which trivially satisfies no-regret, since all servers are identical.

Compute the acceptance probability. Conditioned on sending to a given server, the last time you sent there was i steps ago with probability (1/k)(1 − 1/k)^{i−1}. If you did send there then, it is still busy with probability (1 − 1/n)^i. So

\[\Pr[\text{accepted}] \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k}\Big(1-\frac{1}{k}\Big)^{i-1}\Big(1 - \big(1-\tfrac1n\big)^{i}\Big) \;=\; 1 - \frac{n-1}{n+k-1}.\]

For stability you need this above λ = 1/2, so you need k > n − 1. Total capacity k/n then exceeds (n−1)/n ≈ 1 — double the arrival rate of 1/2.

Now read what that says. There is only one player. There is no competition, no conflict, no externality. The factor of 2 is being paid entirely because the queue is running a sensible learning algorithm that treats identical-looking arms symmetrically and therefore randomises uniformly — and uniform randomisation keeps sending packets to servers it just sent packets to.

Tardos:

"What every self-respecting learning algorithm that I know of will do is uniformly random. You just send a packet to server one. You know what server one is doing — it's trying to serve your packet. Do not send him another one. Round robin works much, much better."

Round robin has the same marginal distribution over servers. It differs only in being negatively correlated across time. And that is worth a factor of 2.

4.14 The first limitation: learning algorithms were designed for a world without memory

She generalises the lesson, and this is the first of her two closing critiques.

No-regret algorithms were invented for two settings: Bayesian (each round is an i.i.d. draw) and worst-case adversarial. What those have in common is that what happened in the past does not change what the future looks like. In a carryover game it does — and the state was created by your own past actions.

"If you actually have knowledge about how things evolved because of what happened in the recent past, then your learning algorithm should take this into account."

There is work in this direction; she does not name it.

4.15 The second limitation: no-regret is myopic — the two-queue example

This one is deeper, and it is where the talk ends up.

The setup, from the podium. Two queues. Two servers: one perfect, serving with probability 1; one bad, serving with probability 0.47.

The unique no-regret outcome: both queues send everything to the perfect server. They split it, so each succeeds with probability 1/2. Deviating to the bad server yields 0.47 < 0.50, so deviating creates regret. No-regret learning will find this and stay.

And it is a disaster. Total system throughput is 1 — only one server is ever used. If the two arrival rates sum to more than 1, both queues grow without bound. Forever.

The stabilising deviation. Suppose one queue moves permanently to the bad server. Total throughput is now 1 + 0.47 = 1.47. If the arrival rates sum to less than that, everything clears. Both queues are better off. The deviator is better off.

And no-regret forbids it, because measured one step at a time, 0.47 < 0.50 and the move looks like a mistake.

Tardos points out two ways the naive comparison is even wrong on its own terms. The deviating queue often finds the bad server empty — no competition there. And when it does occasionally use the perfect server, its packets are now older, so age priority works in its favour and its success rate there exceeds 50%. The 0.47 was never the real payoff of the deviation; it was the payoff of an instantaneous one-step probe.

Which is the diagnosis:

"No regret is too myopic. It's not looking at long-term effects. And in fact, to be honest, even the notation is wrong."

The notation is wrong. Her no-regret inequality wrote u_i(a^t) — utility at time t as a function of the actions at time t. In a carryover game the correct object is

\[u_i^{\,t}\big(a^1, a^2, \ldots, a^t\big)\]

— utility at time t as a function of the entire history. And once you write it that way, "the best single action in hindsight" is the wrong comparison class. The right one is something like "the best single strategy you commit to and stick with all the way through" — which she immediately says "is probably a bit too strong, but something like this."

And she names the failure mode by its familiar name:

"No-regret apparently does something sort of dumb, which I guess we usually call explore–exploit. That is the classical algorithmic strategy: you try something for a second, see how good it is, and then back off right away. Explore, exploit, explore, exploit. And if you don't see it good instantaneously, you back off. You're not watching for long-term strategy."

That is the sentence to take away from the whole lecture.

4.16 What patient players achieve

The vindication is a theorem, and it is the one place where dropping the no-regret assumption improves the guarantee.

Theorem (Gaitonde & Tardos, Virtues of Patience in Strategic Queuing Systems, EC 2021, arXiv:2011.10205). In the same no-buffer model, if patient queues choose strategies that selfishly maximise their long-run success rate — a Nash equilibrium of the long-run sending game, not a learning outcome — then stability is ensured with only

\[\frac{e}{e-1} \;\approx\; 1.58\]

times the centrally required capacity. Strictly better than the 2 that no-regret can guarantee.

Tardos is scrupulous about what this is not:

"What I'm asking is not learning. In fact, what's worse, I don't have a learning algorithm to do this. I ask everyone to find a sending pattern on which they don't want to revise the probabilities of sending, because it's a Nash equilibrium of the sending game."

So the picture is now inverted from where the talk began. In §4.3 she attacked Nash equilibrium as uncomputable and uninformable, and replaced it with no-regret learning. Here the equilibrium concept gives the better bound and there is no algorithm that reaches it. The gap between 1.58 and 2 is exactly the cost of myopia.

She skips the proof (I have not reconstructed it — see §10) and instead offers the two-queue example of §4.15 as the intuition, which is the right pedagogical choice.

4.17 Where she leaves it

Her closing ask, in her own terms:

"Learning players in many interactions actually do reasonably well even with the minimal assumption of no-regret learning. But learning is a bit too simplistic. It would be important to have a better assumption, an alternate behaviour model."

Three requirements for a replacement, and note that all three are hers:

  1. Testable in data — you must be able to check whether real agents satisfy it.
  2. Easy to achieve algorithmically — there must be simple algorithms that guarantee it.
  3. Generates better outcomes — the resulting price of anarchy must be better than what no-regret gives.

No-regret satisfies (1) and (2) and fails (3) in stateful games. Long-run-rate maximisation satisfies (3) and fails (2). The gap is open.

Her final honest note: even her lower-bound example for the e/(e−1) result relies on "a very artificial non-stable solution", so the true constant may be better still. She ran out of time before showing it.

5. The one argument, stated precisely

Theorem (Gaitonde–Tardos, EC 2020). Consider n queues with Bernoulli arrival rates λ_1 ≥ … ≥ λ_n and m servers with success probabilities μ_1 ≥ … ≥ μ_m. Each queue sends at most one packet per step to one server; each server serves at most one packet per step, choosing the oldest among those it receives; unserved packets return to their senders. Suppose there exists η > 0 such that

\[\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_i \;\ge\; \sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_i \qquad \text{for every } k,\]

and suppose every queue uses a strategy whose regret over each window of w consecutive steps is at most φ_δ(w) = o(w) with probability at least 1 − δ, for suitable δ, w depending on η. Then the system is strongly stable: for every r ≥ 0 there is C_r independent of t with E[(Σ_i Q^i_t)^r] ≤ C_r.

Proof architecture.

  1. Replace the process by the coupled dual process tracking only each queue's oldest packet age T^i_t, with gaps between packets drawn as Geometric(λ_i). The two processes have identical stability properties (their Lemma 3.1) and the dual one has the conditional independence the moment bound needs.
  2. Set Φ(T_t) = Σ_i λ_i T^i_t (T^i_t − 1) and Z_ℓ = √(Φ(T_{w·ℓ})) — the potential sampled every w steps.
  3. Verify condition 2 of Pemantle–Rosenthal (bounded p-th conditional moments, p > 2) for Z_ℓ. Straightforward once you are in the dual process.
  4. Verify condition 1 (negative drift once Z_ℓ exceeds a threshold) by the two-case argument of §4.10: for each age cutoff, the best servers were either busy at least half the window — in which case the factor-2 slack in the hypothesis makes clearing outrun arrivals — or idle at least half the window, in which case the no-regret hypothesis, applied with the deviation "always send to that server", forces the queue to have cleared comparably many packets elsewhere. Age priority is what makes "would have been served" true in the second case.
  5. Conclude E[Z^r_ℓ] ≤ C_r by Pemantle–Rosenthal, extend from the sampled times to all t, and translate back through Q^i_t ≈ λ_i T^i_t.

What the factor of 2 is. Not an artifact of the analysis, and not a price of competition. It is the constant in the "busy half the time / idle half the time" dichotomy — and their Theorem 3.3 shows the theorem is false at ½ + o(1), so it cannot be improved for no-regret learners. Their Theorem 2.2 shows that removing age priority destroys the result entirely: no sub-polynomial factor suffices.

6. Do this by hand

6.1 Braess, and the 4/3 (15 minutes, pen)

Take the network of §4.1: 100 cars from A to B. Edges A→C and D→B cost x/100 hours when carrying x cars; edges A→D and C→B cost 1 hour flat.

(a) Confirm the 50/50 split is an equilibrium before the tunnel is added, and compute its cost per car. (b) Add a free instantaneous edge C→D. Show that "everyone takes A→C→D→B" is an equilibrium, and compute its cost. (c) Compute the social optimum on the augmented network, and hence the price of anarchy. (d) Now the point of the exercise. Name, in one sentence, which theorem of optimization Braess's paradox is a counterexample to — and say why it is not actually a counterexample.

Solutions

(a) With 50 on each route, each pays 50/100 + 1 = 1.5. A deviator to the other route makes it carry 51, paying 51/100 + 1 = 1.51 > 1.5. Nobody deviates. Cost 1.5.

(b) With all 100 on A→C→D→B, each pays 100/100 + 0 + 100/100 = 2. Deviating to A→C→B costs 100/100 + 1 = 2 — no gain. Deviating to A→D→B costs 1 + 100/100 = 2 — no gain. It is an equilibrium. Cost 2.

(c) The optimum can simply not use the tunnel: split 50/50 for 1.5 per car. (Adding an edge cannot hurt the optimum, so 1.5 is still optimal.) PoA = 2 / 1.5 = 4/3.

That number is not a coincidence. 4/3 is the tight worst-case price of anarchy over all nonatomic routing instances with affine latency functions (Roughgarden & Tardos, JACM 2002). This tiny network is extremal.

(d) It looks like a counterexample to relaxation monotonicity: enlarging the feasible set cannot worsen the optimal value. It is not one, because the equilibrium is not the optimum of any single objective. It is a fixed point of the best-response map, and fixed points of a parameterised map have no monotonicity guarantee under enlargement of the domain. The optimum did not get worse — it is still 1.5. The fixed point moved.

The moral you can carry: any monotonicity you rely on in optimization needs re-derivation the moment "the solution" becomes "an equilibrium".

6.2 Uniform random versus round robin (20 minutes, pen)

This is Theorem 3 of the buffered paper, and it is a clean geometric-series computation. One queue, arrival rate λ = 1/2. It faces k servers, each with service probability 1/n and a single-packet buffer. At each step the queue sends one packet to a uniformly random server; the server accepts if its buffer is empty, and clears its buffered packet with probability 1/n each step.

(a) Given that you send to a particular server now, what is the probability that the previous time you sent to that same server was exactly i steps ago? (b) Given that, what is the probability that the server is still busy with that earlier packet? (c) Assemble the acceptance probability as a sum over i and evaluate it in closed form. (d) Find the condition on k for the throughput to exceed λ = 1/2, and read off the total capacity k/n you had to buy. (e) Repeat (c) for a round-robin sender, which visits the servers in fixed cyclic order. Compare.

Solutions

(a) Each step you pick that server with probability 1/k independently, so the gap since the last visit is Geometric: Pr[gap = i] = (1/k)(1 − 1/k)^{i−1}.

(b) The buffer clears with probability 1/n per step, so after i steps it is still occupied with probability (1 − 1/n)^i. (Conservatively assuming it was occupied then; that is the bound the paper takes.)

(c)

\[\Pr[\text{accept}] = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac1k\Big(1-\frac1k\Big)^{i-1}\Big(1-\big(1-\tfrac1n\big)^{i}\Big).\]

Split it. The first piece sums to 1. The second is

\[\frac{n-1}{nk}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Big[\big(1-\tfrac1k\big)\big(1-\tfrac1n\big)\Big]^{i-1} = \frac{n-1}{nk}\cdot\frac{1}{1-(1-\frac1k)(1-\frac1n)} = \frac{n-1}{n+k-1},\]

using (1 − 1/k)(1 − 1/n) = 1 − 1/k − 1/n + 1/(nk), so the denominator is (n + k − 1)/(nk). Hence

\[\Pr[\text{accept}] \;=\; 1 - \frac{n-1}{n+k-1}\;=\;\frac{k}{n+k-1}.\]

(d) Need k/(n + k − 1) > 1/2, i.e. 2k > n + k − 1, i.e. k > n − 1. Total capacity is then k/n > (n−1)/n ≈ 1, against an arrival rate of 1/2. A factor of 2, paid by a single player with no competition at all.

(e) Round robin returns to a given server after exactly k steps, so the acceptance probability is 1 − (1 − 1/n)^k. For k = n this is 1 − (1 − 1/n)^n → 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632, against uniform random's k/(n + k − 1) = n/(2n − 1) → 1/2. Round robin gets you comfortably above λ = 1/2 at total capacity k/n = 1; uniform random sits exactly at the boundary and fails.

The whole difference is the variance of the return time. Same marginal distribution over servers, same expected gap of k steps — but uniform random keeps drawing short gaps and wasting sends on servers it has just loaded. Round robin's return time is deterministic. Nothing about "learning" distinguishes them; no-regret is blind to it.

And notice where 1 − 1/e appears. It is not the same constant as the e/(e−1) of §4.16, but it is the same phenomenon wearing a different hat: the cost of independent randomisation where negative correlation was available.

7. What is actually useful to you

You run fleets of agents that share rate-limited resources — API quotas, file locks, a database, a build machine, each other's output. Every one of those is a repeated game with carryover state. This is the closest a talk in this playlist has come to describing your day job in its own vocabulary, and it did not know it was doing so.

7.1 Replace an unverifiable equilibrium assumption with a checkable behavioural contract

The single most reusable move in the lecture is §3.3, and it generalises far past games.

Tardos wanted to say something about a system of independent agents. The classical assumption — "they are at equilibrium" — was unusable on two counts: computing the equilibrium is hard, and no agent has the information needed to be at one. She replaced it with an inequality that is:

  • a property of the observed trajectory, not of an unobserved fixed point;
  • algorithm-agnostic — she never needs to know what any agent is running;
  • falsifiable from logs — you can check it after the fact, and she does, on real bidding data.

That is a design pattern for reasoning about any fleet you did not write all of. When you find yourself assuming your agents "converge", "agree", or "reach a consistent state", ask what weaker, trajectory-level, log-checkable inequality would carry the argument instead. The answer will usually be of the form: no agent persistently did much worse than some fixed simple policy would have done.

And the payoff structure is worth internalising too: the assumption was weaker, so it applied to more systems, and the theorem survived unchanged because the proof had only used the assumption once.

7.2 Price the resource, not the damage

§4.2. Every result in the talk is stated as "how much extra capacity buys off the lack of coordination", never as "how bad is it". 2× traffic, 2× servers, 3× servers with buffers, 1.58× with patient players.

This is the correct shape for a decision you actually have to make. "Our agents waste 40% of the quota through contention" is a complaint. "Provision 2× and you can stop building the coordinator" is a plan — and it is comparing against the real alternative, which is not a perfect world but a central scheduler you would have to write, deploy, and keep correct.

When you next find contention in a fleet, compute the overprovisioning factor that makes the coordinator unnecessary, and compare that to the coordinator's cost. Often the multiplier is small and the coordinator is not.

7.3 The one to implement tomorrow: negative correlation beats independence

§4.13 and exercise 6.2. A single agent, no competition, choosing uniformly at random among identical-looking resources, pays a factor of 2 in capacity against the same agent choosing round-robin. Same marginal distribution. The only difference is that round robin never revisits a resource it just loaded.

Every retry-with-jitter, every random-backend selection, every "pick a random worker" in your harness is the uniform-random algorithm. If the resource has memory of your last request — a warmed cache, an in-flight job, a rate-limit window, a lock you just released — independent sampling is systematically wrong, and the loss is a constant factor, not an epsilon.

Tardos's line is the mnemonic: "You know what server one is doing — it's trying to serve your packet. Do not send him another one."

7.4 Explore–exploit is the wrong loop in a stateful environment

§4.15. The standard bandit loop — try it briefly, measure, back off if it does not look good immediately — is provably correct when the environment is i.i.d. or adversarial, and provably misleading when your own past actions created the state.

The two-queue example is the clean failure: the alternative looks 3 percentage points worse on a one-step probe (0.47 versus 0.50), and taking it is the only thing that keeps the system from diverging. Both effects that make it actually better — reduced contention, and improved priority because your packets became older — only appear after you commit.

The diagnostic to apply to your own harness: when you evaluate an option, does the evaluation destroy the state that would have made it good? If you probe a cold cache, a cold model, a fresh connection pool, or an under-warmed index for one call and revert, you have measured the transient and thrown away the steady state. Anywhere that is true, short-horizon A/B measurement is not conservative — it is biased, and biased in the direction of never switching.

7.5 A queue discipline can be worth more than any amount of intelligence

§4.11 is the most underrated fact in the lecture. Remove "serve the oldest packet" from the model and the theorem does not degrade — it dies. No sub-polynomial capacity factor suffices, even at Nash equilibrium.

The reason is mechanical and it transfers directly. Age priority makes the system reward the agent that has been waiting, which is exactly the agent whose contribution to the potential is largest. It converts individual patience into individual advantage, so selfish learners are pulled toward the globally stabilising behaviour without being told anything.

For your fleets: oldest-first is not a fairness nicety, it is the load-bearing element. If your task queue, retry policy, or scheduler serves whoever asks most recently or most often, no amount of agent sophistication recovers the loss. Change the discipline first, then worry about the agents.

7.6 The connection to the other talks

Wright's plenary (summaries/optimization-theory-practice-wright.md) closes on the theory– practice gap in machine learning: practice runs ahead, theory certifies afterwards. Tardos's lecture is the same gap seen from the other side. Her whole method is to weaken the hypothesis until it describes what practitioners actually deploy — real routers "use much simpler algorithms than what we have good guarantees for" — and then prove something anyway. Wright's answer to the gap is better theory of the practised algorithms; Tardos's is a weaker assumption that all of them satisfy. Both are worth having in the toolkit; the second is cheaper.

And the meta-point she makes and Kontorovich (summaries/shape-of-math-kontorovich.md) makes independently: the interesting question about AI systems is no longer what one of them can do alone. It is what happens when there are many of them and they share something.

9. Self-test

1. State the no-regret condition and say precisely how it differs from Nash.

Nash: u_i(a) ≥ u_i(x, a_{−i}) for every player i and every alternative x — one configuration, no time.

No-regret: Σ_{t≤T} u_i(a^t) ≥ max_x Σ_{t≤T} u_i(x, a^t_{−i}) − o(T) — the same inequality summed over a trajectory, with slack. It compares what you got to the best fixed action in hindsight against the realised play of everyone else.

Differences that matter: no-regret is a property of a trajectory rather than a fixed point; it needs no knowledge of who else is playing; it is achievable by simple algorithms at O(√T) regret; and it is checkable from logs. Nash is none of those.

2. Why do price-of-anarchy bounds automatically apply to no-regret learners?

Because the canonical proof uses the equilibrium hypothesis exactly once, per player, in the single form "player i does not want to deviate to their component s*_i of the optimum". No other deviation is ever invoked. No-regret gives that same inequality in time-averaged form, so you substitute it and the rest of the proof — the "disentanglement" step, which is pure algebra about the game — is untouched. Formally: any (λ,μ)-smooth game has PoA ≤ λ/(1−μ) for Nash, and Roughgarden's extension theorem gives the same bound for all coarse correlated equilibria and hence for the time-average of any no-regret sequence.

Bonus: the learner never needs to know what the optimum is. The no-regret guarantee is universally quantified over comparison actions, so the proof instantiates it wherever it likes.

3. What is the "cheat" in that whole programme?

It assumes no carryover — each round of the repeated game is the same game, unaffected by what happened before, like repeated rock–paper–scissors. Reasonable for morning rush hour day over day (everyone went home overnight). False for millisecond-scale packet routing, for minute-scale traffic, and for budgeted auctions, where spending money today changes the game tomorrow.

4. State the centralised feasibility condition for the queuing model, and explain the k = 1 case in words.

Sort λ_1 ≥ … ≥ λ_n and μ_1 ≥ … ≥ μ_m. Centrally feasible iff Σ_{i≤k} μ_i > Σ_{i≤k} λ_i for every k.

k = 1: the busiest queue can send only one packet per step to one server, so there must be a single server fast enough to absorb it alone — λ_1 < μ_1. Otherwise no scheduling can save it. The general k is the same argument for the k busiest queues, which can occupy at most k servers per step. It is a Hall-type prefix condition, forced by the one-send-per-step constraint.

5. Sketch the negative-drift argument and say exactly where the factor of 2 enters.

Potential = total age of undelivered packets (paper: Φ = Σ_i λ_i T^i(T^i − 1), and Pemantle–Rosenthal applied to √Φ sampled every w steps). Fix an age cutoff, let Q be the queues holding packets older than it, and look at the |Q| fastest servers over a window.

Case A: those servers were busy with old packets at least half the window. Then clearing runs at ≥ ½ Σμ while arrivals run at Σλ, and the hypothesis ½(1−η)Σμ ≥ Σλ makes clearing win. Case B: they were idle at least half the window. Then no-regret fires — those queues hold the oldest packets so they have priority, so "always send there" would have cleared many packets, so by no-regret they cleared comparably many elsewhere.

Either way the potential drops. The 2 is the constant in the "busy half / idle half" dichotomy, and Gaitonde–Tardos Theorem 3.3 shows the result fails at ½ + o(1), so it is tight for no-regret learners.

6. Why is "serve the oldest packet" essential rather than cosmetic?

Case B of the drift argument needs "had the old queue sent there, it would have been served". That is true only because its packets outrank everyone else's. Without timestamps and age priority, Gaitonde–Tardos Theorem 2.2 constructs centrally feasible systems that stay feasible even with λ scaled up by Ω(n^{1/3}), where all queues are at Nash equilibrium every step and the system is still unstable. So no sub-polynomial capacity factor suffices without age priority. The factor 2 is a property of no-regret learning plus a discipline that rewards patience.

7. Why does uniform-random server choice cost a factor of 2 even with a single player?

With k servers each of rate 1/n and single-packet buffers, uniform random sending gives acceptance probability 1 − (n−1)/(n+k−1) = k/(n+k−1). Requiring this to exceed λ = 1/2 forces k > n − 1, so total capacity k/n ≈ 1 against arrival rate 1/2.

The loss is not competition — there is one player. It is that uniform random has high-variance return times and keeps re-sending to a server it just loaded. Round robin has the same marginal distribution but a deterministic return time, giving acceptance 1 − (1 − 1/n)^k, which for k = n is 1 − 1/e ≈ 0.632. No-regret cannot distinguish the two.

8. Explain the two-queue example and what it shows about no-regret.

Two queues, one server of rate 1 and one of rate 0.47. Both queues on the fast server, each succeeding 50% of the time; deviating to the slow server yields 0.47 < 0.50, so no-regret learning locks this in. Total throughput 1, so if arrival rates sum above 1, both queues diverge forever.

If one queue moves permanently to the slow server, throughput becomes 1.47 and the system clears — and the deviator does better than 0.47 in reality, because it often finds the slow server empty and because its aged packets get priority on the fast server when it does use it.

Conclusion: no-regret's comparison class — the best single-step-substitutable fixed action measured against realised play — is the wrong one in a stateful game. The right comparison is a strategy you commit to. No-regret is explore–exploit, and explore–exploit backs off before the benefit materialises.

9. What do patient players buy, and what is the catch?

If queues maximise their long-run success rate rather than satisfying no-regret, stability needs only e/(e−1) ≈ 1.58 times the coordinated capacity, versus 2 for no-regret (Gaitonde–Tardos, EC 2021).

The catch, stated by Tardos from the podium: this is a Nash-equilibrium analysis of the long-run sending game, not a learning result, and no learning algorithm is known that reaches it. The gap between 1.58 and 2 is precisely the price of myopia.

10. What three properties does she ask of a replacement for no-regret?

(1) Testable in data — checkable against what real agents actually do. (2) Easy to guarantee algorithmically — simple algorithms should achieve it. (3) Generates better outcomes — a better price of anarchy in stateful games.

No-regret has (1) and (2), fails (3). Long-run-rate maximisation has (3), fails (2). Nobody has all three. That is the open problem she leaves.
